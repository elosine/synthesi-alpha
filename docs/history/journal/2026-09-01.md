## 133. Step back: the physics of an attacked long tone

Before playing with the enveloper draft, J redirected to first
principles — verbatim, because the formulation carries the whole
problem: "let's take a step back for a second and analyze what
really happens in an attacked long tone. So like an acoustic one.
Like if percussionist is playing a woodblock and, say, a violin,
they both attack, but their attacks are aligned. And then the
violin sustains. Or if you think about a trumpet and just a solo
attack long tone. the attack and then maybe slightly change our
embouchure to sustain the long tone ... can you do a little bit
of an analysis and see how we can better this lab? Maybe some way
to align attacks or... well, first, just think about the physics
of it, and then let's see what I need to do to improve things."

The analysis delivered (summary; full reasoning in the chat):

- Two-player case: onsets land inside the ~20-30 ms fusion
  window, and the tone is ALREADY SOUNDING under the click — the
  click's decay masks the tone's scratchy swell; the tone emerges
  from underneath as the click dies. Fusion = shared onset +
  masking + shared room.
- Solo trumpet: one excitation. Fast rise (5-20 ms) → overshoot
  peak ~+6 dB over sustain → settle to sustain over 100-300 ms →
  sustain. The embouchure change IS the settle; level and
  brightness step down together.
- Common thread: the attack is not BESIDE the tone. The tone
  lives under the attack from time zero, and a settle contour
  connects peak to sustain.

Why the lab's assemblies read as two sounds (four causes):
(1) onset alignment by eye — tens of ms apart; (2) the freeze
voice swells in late and soft (granular window buildup + 
setTimeout gate jitter, no onset envelope) so nothing masks it;
(3) the freeze plays flat — no overshoot-settle, nothing says
"struck"; (4) lesser: spectral handover between the attack slice
and the freeze point may not match.

Offered: A — align-onsets button with a tone-lead (freeze starts
~30 ms early, under the attack); B — strike contour per tone box
(peak +N dB settling to box gain; per-voice gain nodes with
clock-scheduled automation, which also kills the gate jitter);
C = A+B, the recommendation (the full acoustic model); D — 
pair-audition to choose spectrally matching attack/tone material
by ear. Decision pending J.

J's verdict on the Attack Study by ear, verbatim: "while some of

don't wanna get trapped in a rabbit hole and just work on nudging
things here and there. The theory sounds relatively sound ... the

the execution. So I don't know if this is incomplete research or
it's just a coding thing, limitation of web audio, or it's just
incorrect application ... most of the samples have a dominant
sine tone, and it sounds foreign. It sounds like another element
laid on top ... the transient is too short, and, again, not
integrated. the things like timpani and marimba attacks sound
good, but it sounds like a duet with a sine tone everything does.
So maybe there's just a coding error ... Let's get rid of that,
and then I'll reevaluate."

Measured before answering (1.0-1.5 s of four cells): the sustain
is NOT a sine — harmonics 2-10 sit at -5, -9, -12 ... -20 dB, a
1/k sawtooth spectrum. The tell is elsewhere: the fundamental
stands 105-108 dB above the spectral floor (79 dB in the brass
cell, the only one with breath noise and jitter). A real
instrument's sustain has its floor 30-50 dB under the partials —
breath, bow, room, beating of slightly inharmonic partials. A
mathematically clean, phase-locked, static additive tone reads as
electronic: "a sine tone laid on top" is the ear's correct name
for it. Marimba and timpani sounded right ALONE because there the
modal strike is the whole sound; "on the tone" added the sterile
tone underneath — the duet.

Diagnosis: incorrect application of the study's own theory. §133
and the primer say the attack is the excitation of the SAME
resonance that sustains. The code built the two-player model
everywhere — a separate additive tone plus a separate transient,
glued only by a spectral comb on the noise. Not incomplete
research, not the renderer (numpy, not WebAudio): the synthesis
architecture. The "transient too short" note is the same fault
from the other side — a burst pasted on, instead of an excitation
whose onset IS the attack.

Recommended (in chat): change the architecture to source →
resonator. One resonator per family (delay-line string; modal
bank for bar/membrane/bore, which impact_models already has;
formant bank for wind), one excitation whose envelope carries the
attack — impulse or noise burst decaying INTO continuous
excitation. Attack and sustain become one signal by construction.
Before re-rendering 84 cells: a four-cell A/B of sustains alone,
no attack — current additive tone vs continuously-excited modal
bank vs bowed delay line vs pulse train through a bore — so J's
ear picks the sustain model first. Go pending.

J's pick from the Sustain A/B: "lets use the driven modes, and keep
the bowed string around, I have to hear it in context." Then the
foundational question, verbatim: "This is starting to make me
think a bit about just various [sound]-creating devices like
physical modeling or Karplus-Strong ... Can we discuss briefly the
idea of the underlying tone. I'm trying to create attack long
tones, but it strikes me as... these are different ways of
articulating a unified timbre ... So is it the case that a generic
or general purpose sustained tone is going to forcibly sound a bit
... foreign, or, like, from another source. So for example, when I
bring up Karplus-Strong, you can have it plucked, a note that then
continues in the sustain, and it sounds like an attacked long tone
of one timbre ... It sounded like the future approach when we use
the samples would be to try to fuse the timbre of the attack and
the sample sound with filtering, etcetera ... this may be the
issue at foundation."

The discussion delivered (summary): yes — a general-purpose
sustained tone is foreign whenever it is a SECOND SOURCE. Timbre
is not a spectrum; it is a mechanism — a resonator plus a way of
exciting it — and the ear infers the mechanism from the
consistency between transient and sustain (same resonances
ringing, same spectral envelope, coherent modulation). Attack and
sustain are two STATES of one mechanism: how the excitation
begins, and how it continues. Karplus-Strong sounds unified
because the noise burst is inside the loop — the pluck is the
string's own excitation. Bowed/blown extensions are the same loop
with a continuous excitation. Modal synthesis (the driven-mode
bank he chose) is the frequency-domain cousin: resonators as
filters, same principle, and easier to tune to a recording.

So the Attack Study's grid was confused: woodblock, marimba,
timpani, snare are RESONATORS; fp, sfz, scratchy, chiff are
EXCITATIONS / articulations. The physically right grid is
excitation × resonator. And the sample route reframes: a sample
enters either as the resonator's identity (analysis → a modal
bank tuned to the freeze's peaks, then struck/plucked/bowed) or as
the excitation material (the freeze grains drive the resonator,
becoming the sustain's grit while the resonator gives the pitched
identity). The gluing-two-samples route (§136's bridges 1 and 3)
is the fragile one: a two-source fusion trick. J's instinct
("this may be the issue at foundation") is right: choose a
mechanism, not a spectrum. Decision on the rebuild's grid
pending.

## 144. The Impact Study: one attack, noise only (2026-09-02)

J's go on the plan from §143's discussion ("go on that"). The
discussion, summarised for the record: a short rise is necessary
and the least of it. The ear integrates loudness over ~50-100 ms,
so a 1 ms click is sharp but never big. Big needs energy inside
that window (hold 20-60 ms), contrast (15-20 dB over what precedes
and follows), broadband simultaneity (every band starting within
~1 ms — why noise is the archetypal attack), a bright flash that
darkens (highs die first), weight (50-150 Hz for 20-80 ms, ideally
with a pitch drop, as a membrane does), the click + thump split
(contact 1-3 ms high, body 20-60 ms low, aligned within 1-2 ms),
and the hardness law (harder hits are brighter, not just louder).
