# 2026-08-27 — toolchain, two detectors, retrieval, two reframings

A long session. Roughly: recover the ability to make sound at all, build
something that could measure the analysis, fix two detectors and delete a third
thing outright, get retrieval working end to end — and then have the premise
change twice, both times for the better.

---

## 1. Everything was on a branch nobody had merged

Both repos had `main` as a stub and all the real work on
`claude/live-electronics-engine-vt02wv`. The engine's `main` was a 25-byte
README; 13 commits of synthesis work sat unmerged. Both were pure
fast-forwards, so nothing had diverged — it just looked like the work was
missing.

The corpus-extraction toolchain was in the *planning* repo. It moved to the
engine repo, because it feeds conditional accompaniment and two copies of the
same Python would drift while its open bug was unfixed.

**Worth remembering:** the panic ("where did the docs go") was misdirected. The
material was all present, just invisible from `main`.

---

## 2. Three silent failures in the Windows toolchain

The cloud container could render audio. The Windows machine could not, and
none of the three reasons announced itself.

1. `lame` hard-coded as the mp3 encoder. Windows has `ffmpeg` and no lame, so
   every render died *after* writing correct audio.
2. `sclang` invoked bare. The Windows installer puts it in Program Files, not
   on PATH.
3. **The expensive one.** The generated score ended with `);` instead of `)`.
   sclang executing a file parses a top-level `( ... )` as a code block, and a
   trailing semicolon makes it **decline to run the body at all** — no output,
   no error, the process just sits there looking busy. Proved with a three-way
   test: `( … )` runs, `( … );` hangs, no wrapper runs.

Also: Windows paths were being pasted into SC strings, where `\U` and `\b` are
escapes. `C:\Users\...` silently became garbage.

**The pattern worth keeping:** all four failed *quietly*. The audio was correct
and then discarded; the process hung rather than erroring; the path was
malformed rather than missing. Time went into noticing, not fixing.

## 3. The measurement did not exist

Before touching the onset detector, went looking for the test bench that commit
`0cf1562` — *"validated against known ground truth"* — had used.

**It was never committed.** That commit added the doc and the tool and nothing
else. The bench that validated it lived only in the container's working
directory. So did the second bench the handoff described as *"already begun"*.

So: no way to tell whether a change helped. That is why the next several hours
went into building benches rather than fixing anything.

### Three benches, and why two was not enough

- **transient** — clean attacks. The regression guard.
- **sustained** — legato wind and bowed string with vibrato, breath and bow
  noise. Each part seeded with *traps*: vibrato peaks and bow changes that are
  not attacks, recorded separately so a detector can be scored on what it
  should have ignored.
- **metric** — added later, and necessary. The first two are non-metric *by
  construction* (exponentially distributed gaps), which makes them a one-sided
  test: **a detector that called everything aperiodic would score perfectly on
  both.** A bench that can only say "no" is not a test.

Baseline on the unmodified detector: transient F1 0.797, sustained 0.380, and
78–90% of the sustained false positives landing on a recorded trap — the
diagnosis measured rather than assumed.

## 4. The onset detector

### The reverted attempt was right to be reverted, for a reason the handoff did not have

The handoff said log-compressing the spectrogram "went the wrong way" and had
been reverted. Reproduced it: sustained F1 0.380 → **0.291**. Genuinely worse.

The reason: compression on 513 raw FFT bins lets broadband breath noise
contribute 513 small positive changes that sum to more than a real attack.
Compression needs **banding** alongside it. With 96 log-spaced bands it works.
Without them it is actively harmful.

### The change that did the work

**In the log domain, a gain change adds the same constant to every band.** So
subtracting each frame's mean cancels amplitude vibrato and tremolo *exactly*,
while leaving pitch and timbre changes untouched.

Sustained F1 0.397 → 0.637 on that one change.

A small level term (`w_level = 0.2`) went back on top, because a drum hit out of
near-silence genuinely *is* a level event and removing the mean entirely cost
real attacks.

### Dead end: a longer analysis window

The obvious reading of the resolution problem — at NFFT 1024 and 22050 Hz a bin
is 21.5 Hz, so a semitone step at bass clarinet pitch moves 0.2 of a bin and
cannot be seen at all. Obvious fix: a longer window.

    NFFT 1024   sustained 0.630
    NFFT 2048             0.557
    NFFT 4096             0.254

Badly wrong. A 4096 window is 186 ms and the note transitions are 45 ms: the
transition smears across frames and stops being a peak.

**Generalisable:** frequency resolution cannot be bought with window length when
the events are shorter than the window.

### Dead end: phase-deviation detection

Phase is far more sensitive than magnitude to small pitch changes — the same
0.2-bin step is 0.32 radians of extra phase advance per hop. Measured, and true.

Phase deviation *alone* scored 0.365, the worst of everything tried. Adding it
to the shape flux made things worse at every weight above 0.5.

**The reason, obvious only afterwards: vibrato *is* frequency modulation.** A
frequency-sensitive detector is maximally confused by precisely the thing it was
brought in to disambiguate. The complex-domain term survives because it is
anchored to magnitude as well.

### Also wrong, and instructive

The frequency maximum filter — the *standard* vibrato defence — was hurting. At
96 bands one band is 100 cents, so reaching ±1 band absorbs a semitone, which is
the size of the note steps being looked for. The vibrato it was meant to
suppress is ±22–40 cents and never leaves its band. Turning it off gained 0.05.

**A textbook technique applied without checking its scale against the material.**

### Where it ended

Transient 0.797 → 0.977. Sustained 0.380 → 0.615. On real sessions the onset
rate went 3.56 → 2.20/s and intervals sitting on the `min_gap` floor went
5.5% → 1.0%. The failure mode flipped from wild over-detection (tenor: 294
reported against 61 real) to mild under-detection (36 against 61). An
under-count is a missed note; an over-count was a fabricated rhythm.

## 5. The pulse detector, and deleting a feature

### Three faults

1. **The null was not comparable to the signal.** Salience z-scored the
   *continuous flux envelope's* autocorrelation against that of a *sparse
   impulse train*. Different kinds of object; the z-score never meant what it
   claimed.
2. **The fundamental came off the central-lobe shoulder.** "Shortest lag within
   85% of the peak" was meant to avoid reporting a multiple of the beat. But a
   flux envelope's autocorrelation decays smoothly from lag zero, so the
   shortest *allowed* lag sat at **97.9%** of the peak and won every time. Every
   287 BPM reading was the tool reporting its own lower bound.
3. Sub-multiples were not actually handled.

### Then the premise collapsed, usefully

Fixed, the tool still called 14 of 23 real parts metric. Three thresholds and
two null models were tried. Asked J to listen to the four highest-scoring parts.

> no pulse in any, in fact I'm pretty sure nothing ever has steady pulse

**That single answer was worth more than every synthetic bench.** It turned all
23 real parts into known negatives — real playing, ground truth from the person
who made it.

Against it, every null model overlaps:

    null                strongest real   weakest genuinely metric
    uniform                      15.07                       4.29
    ioi-shuffle                   8.64                       2.09
    block-shuffle 4               5.39                       0.13
    block-shuffle 8               4.12                       0.08
    block-shuffle 16              3.50                      -0.95

Stronger nulls pull free improvisation down but pull *genuinely metric* material
down faster — block-shuffling a real pulse preserves it, because consecutive
intervals in metric playing are all one beat long.

**No threshold says yes to a real pulse and no to this archive.**

So the flag was **removed rather than retuned**. It scored 0 true positives and
14 false positives on the only material that matters.

**Principle:** a measurement that is always wrong, with no calibration available,
should be deleted, not re-thresholded. Shipping it with a better number would
have made it harder to notice, not better.

The *tempo* machinery is correct and was kept — on the metric bench all four
tempi come back exactly. It is the **existence** question it cannot answer, not
the value one.

### A correction

Mid-session I reported "8 of 23 parts called metric" as the post-fix state. That
was from an intermediate build; improving onset recall raised salience across
the board and the honest figure was 14 of 23. Recorded because the error was
mine and the corrected number is the one a paper would use.

## 6. Retrieval

`features.py` (5 features per 11.6 ms frame, callable on a bare buffer so the
live side can use the same code), `retrieval.py` (the index), `accompany.py`
(retrieved audio under held-out playing).

A retrieved moment is 2.3× closer to the query than a random one, distributions
clearly separated. Retrieval finds genuinely similar guitar.

### The finding that looked fatal

**Similar guitar does not imply similar drums.** Measured as the spread among
the top-5 retrieved drum moments over the spread among random ones, where 1.00
means the guitar tells you nothing:

    window   vs random   vs same-session random   top-5 all one session
     1.0 s      0.88            0.95                     4%
     4.0 s      0.82            0.90                    20%
     8.0 s      0.71            0.86                    21%
    24.0 s      0.43            0.57                    55%

Two readings, both true. **Accompaniment responds at phrase scale, not gesture
scale** — the plan's 1 s window was simply wrong.

And: a number that improves monotonically deserves suspicion. Checked whether
long windows were merely identifying *which session* — with three sessions
that's easy, and drums within one session are similar for non-musical reasons.
At 24 s, 55% of queries returned all five hits from one session. Partly a
confound. The third column controls for it and the effect survives, but it will
need re-measuring as sessions arrive.

**Doing C2 before C3 was right**: the thing that would have sunk C3 surfaced
before any effort went into C3.

## 7. Two reframings, both from the player, both simplifying

Both arrived from **listening**, not from measurement. In both cases the
measurements had been carefully answering a question that turned out not to be
the question.

### (a) It is not guitar-and-drums. It is anything and anything.

> That just seemed a logical place to start, but it's everything. So if I'm
> playing saxophone, maybe they extract the piano part.

Indexing every part and choosing the answering instrument at *query* time
instead of build time, with no new exports:

    usable sessions      3 of 8  ->  8 of 8
    material            15.7 min ->  45.3 min
    indexed windows       3,518  ->  26,911
    instruments               2  ->  9
    directed pairings         1  ->  46

Retrieval improved with more to choose from: 2.3× → 3.1×.

A whole class of measurement — the prediction ratio above — became irrelevant,
because it was only ever a problem under the assumption that one right answer
existed.

### (b) The pattern recognition is not the point. Timbre is.

> maybe the pattern recognition isn't the main thing to go for. It's more about
> making the sounds themselves interesting. […] the main crux is timbres that
> change in time — not just volume, but the spectrum changing over time, the way
> a flam will change dramatically.

This **inverts what the archive is for**. Everything up to here treated it as
material to retrieve and play back. This treats it as a library of *control
signals*: what is valuable is not the ride cymbal but the way a ride cymbal gets
darker and noisier as it decays — a shape that can steer a filter, a grain
density or an FM index, and that has nothing to do with cymbals.

And it makes the archive far larger. Every onset in every part is a gesture:
tens of thousands already, against 26,911 retrieval windows.

Crucially, J was explicit that this is **not** matching:

> I wouldn't try to extract the feature set of the ride cymbal playing and then
> try to match all that exact same sound, but have an abstraction where the ride
> data has a certain profile […] used in generating interesting electronic
> sounds.

## 8. Gestures as control signals

`timbre.py`: one onset, 450 ms, 24 frames, ten dimensions. `flatness` carries
the distinction being asked for:

    piano 0.029     archtop 0.192     drums 0.415

A fourteen-fold spread. "A drum profile" is a real, extractable thing.

Each gesture stored twice. **`curve`** keeps real values; **`shape`** scales
each dimension to its own range within that gesture, discarding absolute pitch
and level and keeping only motion. `shape` is the abstraction — it is what makes
a piano's trajectory usable on something that sounds nothing like a piano.

The routing to synthesis parameters is **a choice, not a finding**. Nothing is
fitted or learned. `--swap` re-routes centroid to the FM index and flux to the
cutoff, which sounds nothing like the source, and exists to keep that visible.
If the analogous routings ever start to feel obligatory, that is a sign of
sliding back into thinking about reproduction.

**Named but unbuilt:** the meso timescale (2–20 s), which the granular engine's
density-independent-of-loudness design wants two orthogonal control curves for;
extraction modes beyond trajectories (statistical, rate, derivative, joint); and
driving *sample processing* rather than synthesis.

## 9. The meso timescale — built, 2026-08-27

Named as the most valuable unbuilt thing in §4 and then built the same day.
`corpus/phrases.py` segments; `corpus/render_phrases.py` drives the cloud engine.

### Why this scale is the one that pays

`analyze_session --frames` had been writing a continuous five-feature stream at
11.6 ms since C1 and **nothing read it as a control signal**. Retrieval sampled
it into fixed windows and discarded the shape; `timbre.py` looks at 450 ms. The
phrase-scale motion in between was measured and unused.

It matters because of a commitment made in the cloud engine long before any of
this: **density stays independent of loudness**, so a cloud can evaporate to
isolated points while growing louder. The synth compensates the sqrt(overlap)
power sum that would otherwise make density *be* loudness.

That design wants two independent control curves over several seconds, and the
frame features already contain exactly those two. Not proxies — `density_hz` is
events per second and so is grain density.

### The result worth writing down

    1,289 phrases, 115.2 minutes, median 4.4 s

    density and loudness moving in opposite directions:
      thins while getting louder       170   13%
      thickens while getting quieter   140   11%

**24% of phrases move the two apart.** The engine's central premise, adopted
from Roads on theoretical grounds, turns out to be a measured property of this
playing. That is a considerably better argument for the design than the one it
was built on, and it was available for free once anything read the frame
features at the right timescale.

### How the boundaries are found

Fixed windows would cut across gestures. The requirements doc had asked for
phrase boundaries from "density and activity discontinuities", so: novelty is
the distance between the mean feature vector of the 1.5 s before each frame and
the 1.5 s after, on z-scored features; boundaries are the peaks, thinned to a
2.5 s minimum and split at 20 s.

A simplified Foote novelty — no checkerboard kernel, because at this timescale
the plain before/after contrast finds the same boundaries and the
self-similarity matrix is mostly expense.

### How a curve becomes a cloud

`roads-cloud` interpolates each parameter from a start to an end value across
`cloudDur`, so a phrase of 16 trajectory points becomes **15 clouds butted end
to end**, each carrying one segment. The SynthDef is used exactly as designed
rather than worked around, and the motion is continuous.

Rendered four ways, including the A/B that matters: the same six divergent
phrases with compensation on and off, so what the decoupling buys is audible
against real playing rather than argued for.

## 10. A routing catalogue, and processing the recordings

Asked for a broad sweep of what the feature sets could be translated into -
reverb, filter sweeps, freeze and extension effects, ring modulation, granular
processing, direct sample playback - and named two SuperCollider plugins
(Greyhole, and a ring modulator by the same authors).

### What is actually installed

`Greyhole` and `JPverb` are Julian Parker's Faust ports and live in
**sc3-plugins**, which is not installed. Core SuperCollider 3.14.1 turns out to
cover most of what was named: `GVerb`, `FreeVerb`, `MoogFF`, `Ringz`,
`PV_MagFreeze` (the freeze), `Warp1`, `GrainBuf`, `PV_BinShift`, `PV_MagSmear`,
`Convolution2` — 419 UGens, 35 of them spectral. Ring modulation is
multiplication and needs no plugin at all.

I could not identify the ring modulator by the same authors and said so rather
than guessing; Greyhole and JPverb I am confident about.

sc3-plugins was **not** installed unasked — it means downloading a release, and
that is J's decision. Nothing built here depends on it.

### The catalogue

[`docs/plan/feature-routing.md`](../plan/feature-routing.md). Nine technique
families, each with the features that naturally steer it and *why that pairing
rather than an arbitrary one*.

The organising idea, which is what makes it more than a list: **a routing is
worth having when a technique has a parameter that has always been set by hand
and arbitrarily, and a measured trajectory can set it instead with some claim to
being musical.**

The clearest instance is reverb. Decay time and damping are almost always chosen
by ear, and every percussive event in the archive already carries a measured
answer — how long it rang, and how much brightness it lost while ringing.

### Built the same day

`synths/feature-chain.scd` and `corpus/process_sample.py` implement the top four
routings, and this is the **first tool here that processes the recordings**
rather than synthesising alongside them:

    resonator bank   gains   <- the four band energies
    ring modulation  carrier <- centroid,  depth <- flatness
    freeze           trigger <- low flux
    reverb           time    <- decay slope,  damping <- darkening

Freeze-on-low-flux is the neatest of the four: the measurement that says nothing
is changing triggers the effect that stops change. It needed one threshold.

Two modes. Steering audio by its **own** features is legible but slightly
circular — the processing agrees with the material because it was measured from
it. `--drive-part` steers it with a **different instrument's** features from the
same instant, which the sample-exact alignment makes possible. That is one
player's motion applied to another's sound, and it is not matching or
reproduction of any kind.

Measured on 25 s of drums from 2025-04-04a:

    steered by its own features    reverb 0.6-8.9 s, frozen 12% of the time
    steered by the piano           reverb 0.6-9.0 s, frozen 34%

Frozen nearly three times as often when the piano drives it, because the piano
sits still more. The freeze is reporting something true about the *other*
instrument.

## 11. The flow was right, the material was not

J on the phrase-scale renders, having listened:

> I like that thing "the other direction". I like the way the flow of it, the
> waves of it — it's very natural, kind of wave-like motion. But then the
> underlying sounds are sort of unchanging, they're sort of the same. […] the
> sound design needs working. It lacks richness.

A precise complaint, and correct. `roads-cloud` uses `GrainSin` — a sine grain,
and it stays one for as long as the cloud lasts. The phrase-scale motion on top
of it is good *because it comes from real playing*; the material underneath had
never been given the same treatment.

### The brief, which came with the complaint

> one of the things I enjoy about my piano playing is I'm using muted strings,
> I'm using harmonics, I'm using plucked strings, I'm using regular playing and
> striking the strings — and so in a short span of time, in a very rhythmically
> active way, I'm switching rapidly between timbres.

So: five modes of sound production, switched rapidly, inside a flow that already
works. **The structure is what is borrowed, not the sound** — none of the modes
is trying to be a piano, and the point would be lost if they were.

    struck     six partials, medium decay        the ordinary note
    plucked    brighter, faster, slightly stiff  more attack, less body
    muted      two partials, 60 ms, lowpassed    a thud with a pitch in it
    harmonic   one high partial, slow attack,    no fundamental at all
               long ring
    inside     noise through inharmonic          no pitch to speak of
               resonators

`synths/string-grain.scd` is one grain with enough parameters to be any of them;
`corpus/render_strings.py` holds the five parameter sets. Keeping the modes in
Python means a sixth costs a dict entry rather than a SuperCollider edit, and
they sit next to the reasoning.

### What the switching rate does

`--runs` sets how many grains a mode holds before another is chosen. Rendered at
1, 3 and single-mode so the range is audible rather than asserted:

    one mode only          404 grains, all struck
    runs of 3              struck 103, plucked 93, muted 76, harmonic 66, inside 66
    runs of 1              a different mode every grain

At 1 it is a shuffle; at 8 it is monochromatic again. Two to four is where it
starts to sound like someone making decisions. Mode weights are biased by the
trajectory — noisy stretches favour `inside` and `muted`, bright ones favour
`harmonic` and `plucked` — but never exclusively, because unpredictability is
most of what makes rapid switching read as intentional.

### The lesson

The phrase-scale work assumed that if the *motion* came from real playing, the
result would be musical. It half worked: the motion is the good part and the
complaint was entirely about what was moving.

**Two timescales were being borrowed from the corpus and one was being invented
by default.** Density and loudness came from measurement; the grain was a sine
because nobody had chosen otherwise. The fix was not more analysis but noticing
which parameter had never been given a source.

Worth checking the same question against everything else built today: which
parameters are measured, and which are simply whatever they were left at?

## 12. The same idea again, one layer up

> The processing-the-recording set of sound files are really starting to get
> there, so we'll continue in that direction. Maybe just a greater variety of
> processing and more rapid change again. The piano example I think is the good
> one.

"Again" is the word that matters. The grain modes had just fixed monochromatic
*material* by switching rapidly between five ways of making a sound; the same
complaint now applied to *processing*, which was modulating four stages
continuously and therefore always producing the same kind of sound, filtered
differently.

**Continuous modulation gives motion. Switching gives variety. They are not
substitutes, and the phrase-scale work needed both.**

### Eight configurations

`synths/process-chain.scd` widens the chain to ten stages;
`corpus/render_processing.py` holds eight configurations of it, named for what
they do to the material rather than which stages they use:

    open      barely touched; resonance and a little air
    ring      heavy ring modulation, sidebands off the centroid
    shifted   frequency shift - harmonic material comes out inharmonic
    frozen    spectral hold, long reverb; the moment stops
    wash      smeared and drowned; no attacks survive
    points    spectral gate; only the loudest bins
    combed    comb delay at a measured inter-onset interval
    crushed   driven and filtered, the aggressive one

Every stage is a wet/dry mix rather than a branch, so configurations overlap at
the crossfades and a switch is not a cut.

### Two rates at once

This is the structural point, and it is what the grain-mode work did not have.

**Which configuration** is fast — `--hold` seconds on average, drawn from an
exponential so changes are irregular rather than metronomic. **What the
continuous parameters do inside it** stays slow, driven from the source as
before. Cutoff still tracks centroid, reverb still inherits decay; what changes
quickly is which of those are audible at all.

Rendered at three rates on the same 30 s of piano:

    every 0.5 s   66 changes
    every 1.0 s   31 changes, all eight configurations appear
    every 4.3 s    7 changes - settles into one thing, the original complaint

### One detail worth keeping

`combed` runs its delay at the **measured median inter-onset interval of the
source**. Steered by the piano that is 459 ms; steered by the drums, 267 ms. The
delay repeats at the rate the player was actually playing at — not a tempo,
since this archive has none, but a measured interval, and a parameter that would
otherwise have been guessed.

## 13. Equal amplitude is not equal loudness

Listening notes on the five grain modes, offered as observations rather than a
request:

> the every single grain is actually not bad. […] the harmonic ones tend to
> dominate. […] they have kind of an inharmonic sound to them and so they
> dominate the texture, so there's a lot of interesting stuff going on in more
> percussive things, but the most prominent layer is this inharmonic longer tone
> and that sounds rather synthesized. […] the granular stuff works when the
> grains are short and percussive, and there was a lot of interesting things
> going on under that inharmonic synth layer.

Two separate faults in that, one of them a real bug.

### The bug: energy, not probability

`harmonic` was not being chosen too often — it was 63 grains of 426, the least
frequent of the five. It rang for **1.25 s** while the others ran 0.06–0.42 s.

At equal amplitude, a grain lasting twenty times longer carries roughly twenty
times the energy. So the mode nobody chose most was nonetheless the loudest
thing in the texture by a wide margin, and everything shorter was playing
underneath it.

The fix is the correction the cloud engine already makes for grain density, for
exactly the same reason — incoherent events sum in **power**, not amplitude:

    amplitude *= sqrt(reference_decay / mode_decay)

    struck    0.42s   x0.85
    plucked   0.22s   x1.17
    muted     0.06s   x2.24
    harmonic  0.70s   x0.65
    inside    0.30s   x1.00

`roads-cloud.scd` had this correction in it from the day it was written, with a
comment explaining why. Nothing carried it across to the grain modes, because
the modes were built as a palette rather than as a summing problem.

### The taste fault: purity

At `tilt` 2.4 with `inharm` 0, `harmonic` was very nearly a pure sine four
octaves up — the most synthetic sound available, which is what "sounds rather
synthesized" was picking out. Now `tilt` 1.55 with `inharm` 0.28: partials
audible, slightly stretched, and a little attack noise.

### And a judgement of mine that was wrong

The docstring said switching every grain "is too much" and that two to four was
where it sounded like decisions. J's read: *the every single grain is actually
not bad*. I had written a preference into a comment as though it were a finding.
Rendered at `runs 1` from here on, and the docstring corrected.

**Generalisable:** a palette assembled one element at a time will be unbalanced
in whatever dimension nobody was thinking about. Here it was duration, which is
not a property of any single mode but of the comparison between them.

## 14. Cross-driving is the mechanism

> The steered-by-the-drums processing is the most interesting. I definitely
> think this is the direction we should go in, where one instrument's — or even
> synthesis — is being processed through the features of another instrument.
> This is giving that sort of the piano model, where I maybe just play a phrase
> over two or three seconds but change timbre in a flow, you know, organic way.

The single most consequential sentence of the day, because it names the
mechanism rather than an outcome: **audio from one source, control from
another.** Everything built since C1 turns out to have been assembling the parts
for it.

It also explains why the timbre work and the accompaniment work were converging.
Retrieval asked "what should answer this?" and the answer was weakly determined.
Cross-driving asks "what should this *become*?" and takes the answer from a
second player who was in the room at the time. The sample-exact alignment that
the whole export procedure exists to guarantee is what makes it possible, and it
was originally guaranteed for retrieval, which turned out to need it less.

### Two developments

**Switching now lands on the driver's accents.** The configurations were
changing on an exponential timer, which is irregular but not *motivated*. They
now change at the driver's own onsets — filtered to the stronger half, because a
configuration that changes on a strong event reads as a decision and one that
changes on a weak event reads as a glitch. Same piano, same drums, rendered both
ways for comparison.

This is the difference the word "organic" was pointing at. Irregularity was
already there; what was missing was that the changes agreed with something.

**Synthesis can be the material.** `--audio` accepts any file, so the five-mode
grain output can be the thing processed, steered by a real instrument. Rendered
steered by drums and by piano: same synthesis, two different readings of it.

    steered by drums   30 changes, comb at 255 ms, points 36% crushed 18%
    steered by piano   18 changes, comb at 360 ms, combed 30% points 27%

The drums produce more changes and more aggressive configurations; the piano
produces fewer and washier ones. Neither was chosen — both fall out of what
those players were doing.

### What this reframes

The five rungs in `corpus-accompaniment.md` were about *what answers the live
playing*. This is a different axis and probably the more productive one: not
what plays, but what shapes what plays. A synthesised layer, a retrieved
fragment and a raw recording are all just material; the interesting decision is
which player's motion is applied to it.

## 15. The stage between listening and responding

> my sound becomes data and then that data gets transformed in a number of
> different ways, and then that's used to process the electronic sounds. […]
> it's not like your traditional live processing where I play sound and then it
> gets processed somehow.

The reference is Risset's *Duet for One Pianist* (1989) — a pianist plays a
Disklavier, the computer reads the MIDI, transforms it, and drives the same
piano's solenoids. Gathered into
[`docs/reference/risset-duet.md`](../reference/risset-duet.md).

    pianist  ->  MIDI  ->  [ TRANSFORM ]  ->  MIDI  ->  Disklavier

`Narcisse` inverts pitch intervals — a fifth up reflected as a fifth down.
`Stretch` multiplies them, so a chromatic run comes back whole-tone. `Fractals`
returns six notes at 13, 26, 39, 52, 65 and 78 semitones above the input.
`Echo` returns the material transposed in pitch and tempo at various delays.

Each is **one operation, statable in a sentence**. The interest comes from a
legible transformation meeting unpredictable input — not from the sophistication
of the transformation.

### What was missing here

Everything built up to now used the measured data **as measured**. The driver's
brightness drove brightness, its density drove density. Faithful, and the least
interesting thing available.

`corpus/transforms.py` adds the middle stage. Ten operations on a trajectory:

    invert      where it rose, fall                    Narcisse
    retrograde  backwards
    stretch     exaggerate every departure from centre Stretch
    flatten     the opposite
    lag         the response arrives after the cause   Echo
    fractal     the same contour at three timescales   Fractals
    crosswire   each dimension driven by another
    derivative  rate of change rather than value
    quantise    reduced to a few states

Transforms operate on the **normalised** trajectory and are rescaled back, so
`invert` means "where this rose, fall" rather than "subtract from 12000 Hz", and
one operation is meaningful across dimensions with incompatible units.

### Two decisions worth recording

**Inversion is a choice here in a way it is not for Risset.** His data is
symbolic and discrete; inverting a pitch interval is exact. A brightness curve
has no natural axis to reflect around. Reflecting about each dimension's own
mid-range within the window keeps it in its own register, which is almost always
what is wanted — reflecting about the global range would send a quiet passage to
the top of the register.

**Switch points are not transformed.** The configurations still change on the
driver's *real* accents; only their character is transformed. So the rhythm of
the response stays true to what was played and the content of it is inverted or
delayed or fractalised. Transforming both would have made the response
untethered, which is a different piece.

### The risk

Risset's output was the *same instrument*, which is why an inversion reads as an
inversion. Here the output can be anything, and an inverted contour driving an
unrelated timbre may not read as inverted at all — it may just read as different.
Whether the transformations are *audible as transformations* is a listening
question and nothing measured will answer it.

## 16. Not a transformation. A transformation engine.

A correction to how I had read the Risset reference, from J:

> I think I was using his approach less literally but more conceptually, just
> adding that transforming layer. […] that transformation doesn't have to be so
> literal like retrograde or falling versus something like that, but could be a
> number of different transformations — or could be meta, like the drums
> transformed by a guitar sample and then that one transformed by a bass
> clarinet, and then that transforming the original piano. […] the Risset is
> just the way to say that we could build a transformation engine in between.

I had built `transforms.py`: nine scalar operations on one trajectory. Correct
as far as it went, and much too small. **A step in the chain can be another
player.**

That changes the size of the space entirely. With nine instruments and five
binary operations, the archive supplies the *operators* as well as the operand —
and none of it needs new material.

### `corpus/steer.py`

Steps separated by `|`, read left to right. The first names where the steering
starts; everything after transforms it.

    drums                                    plain
    drums | invert                           one unary operation
    drums | blend archtop 0.4                two players averaged
    drums | warp archtop                     the guitar decides the pace
    archtop | modulate drums                 both must be active
    drums | invert | blend archtop | warp piano    three players, four steps

Binary operations: `blend`, `modulate`, `gate`, `warp`, `replace`.

`warp` is the one that behaves least like arithmetic. It does not mix the two
players — it lets one control **when** the other is read from, so a busy passage
in the controlling part races through the whole of the other's contour and a
still one dwells on a single moment. One player supplies shape, the other
supplies time.

### One decision the chain forced

The continuous steering comes from the chain, but the configuration switches
still need events to land on, and a chain has no single obvious owner of those.

The **head** of the chain gets them: the part the chain starts from. It is the
player the response is a response to, and everything after it in the chain
modifies rather than replaces that. So `drums | blend archtop | warp piano`
still changes on the drummer's accents; what it changes *to* comes from all
three.

That keeps the same separation established earlier — the rhythm of the response
stays true to one player, the character of it can come from anywhere.

### What this makes possible that was not before

The interesting configurations are not the ones with more steps. `archtop |
modulate drums` is two players and one operation, and it means something
specific: *steer only where both of them were active.* That is a musical
statement about an ensemble, made out of measurements, and there was no way to
express it an hour ago.

## 17. One parameter group per instrument, and a way to tune by ear

Two directions, given together:

> many different parameters can be controlled by different instruments […] a
> guitar part could essentially be in charge of the reverb […] another
> instrument could govern spatialisation, like mono versus wide and Leslie type
> of action, or a granular swirl.

> the thing to dive in on is really getting the details of these effects and
> processing right […] finding the best limits and the best ranges of those
> things, and we'll do that by ear.

The first is architecture. The second is the more important one, and the reason
the first is worth having.

### The ensemble runs the console

`corpus/render_multi.py`. Six groups, each taking its own part or chain:

    switch    which configuration, and when it changes    the conductor
    filter    cutoff, resonance, high-pass
    reverb    time, damping, wet, room size
    colour    ring modulation, frequency shift, drive
    space     width, swirl rate and depth, position
    texture   resonator bank, comb, smear, gate, freeze

`switch` is not like the others: it supplies the accents everything changes on,
and the weighting that picks the configuration. The rest only fill in dials once
that decision is made. So one player still runs the piece and the others run
departments.

Previously every parameter came from one driver, which meant **every parameter
moved together**. That is why it read as one thing being modulated rather than
several things happening. Splitting the groups costs nothing, because the
recordings were already aligned.

Spatialisation was added to the chain for this: width by decorrelation (one side
delayed by a wandering few milliseconds, so the centre is not hollowed the way an
anti-phase trick would), and a swirl that rotates the pair — slow and shallow it
is a Leslie, fast and deep it is closer to a granular scatter. Width comes from
spectral spread and swirl from flux, neither of which is a stretched metaphor:
both are the same quantity read in a different domain.

### The tuning problem, stated properly

**Every number in `MODES` was chosen by me, and none of them was ever heard in
isolation.** They were always auditioned while a driver was moving, which is
precisely the condition under which it is impossible to tell whether a setting
is good or whether a good moment happened to land on it.

`corpus/audition.py` renders one configuration at six settings on the *same four
seconds* of audio, back to back, **with nothing feature-steered**. What is heard
is the parameter and nothing else. Each mode has a default sweep on the
parameter that mostly decides whether it works:

    open resMix · ring rmMix · shifted fsMix · frozen revMix
    wash smear · points gate · combed combFb · crushed shapeMix

Ranges are deliberately wide enough to be clearly wrong at both ends, because a
sweep that is all plausible tells you nothing about where the edges are.

### Why this is the right shape of tool

It moves the judgement to where it belongs. I can build the mechanism and I can
make the ranges wide; I cannot hear whether `wash` at smear 14 is better than at
20, and no measurement will tell me. The tool produces the comparison and the
answer comes back as a number.

That is a better division of labour than the one that has been running all day,
where I chose values and J corrected them after hearing them in context.

## 18. What the corpus is actually for

The clearest statement of the project's thesis so far, and it arrived as an
aside rather than a request:

> one of my criticisms about electronic music, or one of the challenges, has
> been to capture natural flow. Oftentimes when you're using things like LFOs,
> or even granular synthesis algorithms, it's hard to have this sort of smooth
> flow and change of things. And so I think our rhythmic database — this
> analysis of my playing and the onsets — really is that missing link. It
> provides that rhythmic flow, what you called the pace. […] And also timbral
> change, I didn't mention. So that very human flow, not only applied to rhythm,
> onsets and dynamics, but also to all the other parameters.

That reframes everything built today as one idea. The corpus is not a sample
library, nor a set of features, nor even a control-signal library. It is **a
source of motion that no oscillator can produce**, and the point of the
transformation engine is to find more places to spend it.

It also explains why "read at the piano's pace" landed — that phrase named the
thing the whole apparatus exists to supply.

### The audit that followed

If that is the thesis, the obvious question is where a machine is still doing
what the playing could do. Three places, all of them mine:

**`SinOsc.kr(swirlRate)`** — the spatial swirl was a sine LFO. Its *rate* came
from playing and its *shape* came from an oscillator, which is precisely the
tell being complained about: it arrives on time forever. The position is now fed
a real contour, and the oscillator survives only as a residual that defaults to
zero.

**`LFNoise2.kr(0.13)`** — the stereo decorrelation delay wandered on machine
noise. Now it wanders with the driver.

**`rng.exponential(1 / density)`** — and this was the worst. The grain scheduler
took the measured density and used it to drive a **Poisson process**. So it
reproduced the *average rate* of the playing and threw the placement away — the
one number was borrowed and the flow, which is the thing the corpus has that an
algorithm does not, was discarded and regenerated from a dice roll.

Grains now land on the times those parts were actually struck. On six phrases
that is 108 real onsets where the Poisson version produced 426 events at the
same average rate: four times as many, and none of them where anyone played.
`--subdiv` fills in between real onsets for density without inventing the pulse.

### The generalisable form

**Borrowing a statistic from the playing is not the same as borrowing the
playing.** A rate, a mean, a distribution — those can all be reproduced by a
generator, and reproducing them is what makes something sound synthetic despite
being derived from real material. What cannot be reproduced is *where the events
actually fell*, and that is the only part worth having.

Every remaining `rng.` in the renderers is now a question rather than a
convenience. Some are legitimate — which phrase to pick, which pitch from a
scale. The ones that decide *when* something happens are all suspect.

## 19. Two layers, and the difference between superimposing and articulating

The last idea of the session, and the one that joins the two halves of the day:

> the different performances controlling the different parameters is really
> good. I would call that the base material layer, and then have the granular
> articulation layer on top of that. […] so then you have the timbral material,
> space, everything being continuously transformed, and then that being
> articulated by the granular layer.

### The decision that made it work

"Superimpose" and "articulated by" pull in different directions, and the second
is the stronger reading. Two layers playing at once are two things. **A layer
that reads out of the other is one thing with a rhythm imposed on it.**

So `synths/grain-articulate.scd` does not generate anything. Every grain is a
window cut out of the processed base *at the moment already playing* — the
reverb, the crushing, the spatial motion are all in the material before the
grain takes hold of it.

That turns the five modes from timbres into **grips**:

    muted      50 ms, lowpassed        a stab at the material
    plucked    140 ms, bright          a flick
    struck     300 ms                  an ordinary grip
    harmonic   900 ms, high-passed     lifts the upper region and holds it
    inside     180 ms, brittle top     scrapes across the surface

`string-grain.scd` still exists and still makes sound; it is a different layer
for a different purpose. This one only takes hold.

### Three players in one render

The layering makes room for more of them than any previous stage:

    base steered by     drums (switch, colour) + archtop (reverb, space)
    grains placed by    the drummer's real onset times
    grips chosen by     the archtop's brightness and flux

None of that required new material. It required noticing that the parts are
aligned and that a grain does not have to synthesise.

### `--bed`, and why it is the interesting parameter

How much untouched base survives underneath. At 1.0 the grains are an overlay on
a continuous layer; at **0.0 the base exists solely as something to cut from**
and is never heard directly. Rendered at both ends.

That parameter is the whole question the idea raises. Superimposition and
articulation are not two techniques — they are the two ends of one control, and
where it should sit is a musical decision that nothing measured will settle.

### Where this leaves the shape

The engine now has, in one render: material chosen by a player, transformed by
several players in separate parameter groups, articulated at a fourth player's
rhythm, with the grip of each articulation chosen by a fifth reading. Every one
of those is measured off real playing rather than generated, which was the point
J made in §18 and which this is the fullest expression of so far.

## 23. Sound images: the recipe is the documentation

> during this process, I want to document and track the decisions we make and
> the way we did it. And then the ranges and everything. So then when we build
> this pleasing sound, we've collected the processes that we use to build that
> sound and then we can extract those and apply those as rules […] until it
> becomes a generator.

A different way of working from every other renderer here. Those choose for
themselves — a mode pool, a switching rule, a weighting — and the choosing is
the interesting part. In a sound image **nothing is chosen**: the carrier is
assembled by hand and every move after it is one decision made by ear.

The design that follows from his last sentence: **the recipe file is the
documentation.** `images/imgNN.py` lists the carrier and the moves with J's own
words for why each is there; building it emits `images/imgNN.md`.

The log reports the **measured** range, not the declared one. A move that says
"cutoff 200→9000 from the drums' brightness" may only ever reach 3000, and the
log says so, and flags any driver that crossed less than 55% of its own range.
That number is what makes the eventual generator possible — a move that keeps
earning its place can be lifted out and applied to material it was not made for,
and knowing what it actually did is the difference between transplanting a rule
and transplanting a guess.

### Two bugs the first render exposed, both fatal to the method

- **7 seconds of trailing silence.** The score leaves room for reverb to ring
  out, which is silence when nothing is ringing.
- **The render came out 9 dB below the carrier** — chain gain, `amp 0.7` plus
  the dry path.

Neither matters for a single render. Both are fatal to a method whose entire
business is judging one version against the next: a change in loudness is heard
as a change in quality. Output is now trimmed and levelled, with the gain
written into the log so it is not hidden.

### The confusion worth writing down

The shell reported `%LOCALAPPDATA%\SuperCollider` existing with contents.

`LessKeys`, `CoverMe`). BatUGens, by the same author, is `Coyote` (an onset
detector), `MarkovSynth`, and buffer position generators.

## 25. Four sessions, two microphones, and arithmetic

The new exports put the session name on the *folder* rather than inside every
filename. That is the better layout — nothing to recover, and no dependence on a
project name never happening to contain a track name. `parse_stems.discover()`
now walks both.

Twelve sessions. Onset rates on the new material: **1.7 to 2.9 per second**,
inside the band established when the detector was rebuilt, on material it was
never tuned against.

### The microphone thing

One session records the bass clarinet on two mics, `Bcl ORTF` and `Bcl Rode`.
`load_part` averaged everything matching a part, which is harmless for the two
channels of a coincident pair and **not harmless for two mics in different
positions**: the same sound arrives at each at a different time, and summing
them comb-filters it — a fixed notch every few hundred Hz that would then have
been heard and attributed to the processing.

It now keeps whichever mic has the most audio.

### The arithmetic

The 90-second carrier came out at 82.8 s. Each snippet after the first overlaps
its predecessor by one crossfade, so it contributes `dur − xfade` to the
finished length; counting raw durations overcounts by `(n−1) × xfade`, which for
22 splices at 0.35 s is exactly the 7.7 s that went missing. Fixed at the source
rather than by padding the target.

### The decision inside the carrier

The randomness happens **once**, and the result is written into the recipe as
25 explicit slices. A carrier re-rolled on every render cannot be judged,
because the thing being listened to changes underneath the listening. Written
down, "that third one is wrong, find another" is a real instruction.

Instruments are cycled rather than drawn uniformly — piano appears in most
sessions, so uniform draws would have produced a mostly-piano carrier — and
every window is checked against the analysis for the player actually playing,
because silence in a carrier is a hole rather than restraint.

## 28. Grouping the effects by what the ear notices

> the mental image I'm using is that... let's say you're processing segments of
> the carrier in time. So the first five seconds or the next ten, etcetera. then
> I would like to hear a variety.

A grouping problem before a scheduling one, and the obvious grouping is the
wrong one. **By DSP category the groups are useless here**: a lowpass and a
highpass are the same category and sound nothing alike, while a long reverb and
a spectral freeze are different categories and both just mean "it went on".

Eight families, grouped by effect on the listener:

    OPEN        barely touched - the reference the rest are heard against
    COLOURED    the same sound through a different window
    RUNG        pitch that was not there before
    DETUNED     harmonic material made inharmonic
    RUINED      the surface broken
    SCATTERED   time broken into pieces
    SUSPENDED   time stopped
    SPACED      distance and width rather than colour

### Coordinates, not a list

A flat list of eight does not answer "what comes next". Each family sits at a
point on two axes - **time** (broken to continuous) and **surface** (clean to
ruined) - and contrast is the distance between them. SCATTERED to SUSPENDED is
0.89; OPEN to COLOURED is 0.16 and will not carry a change on its own.

Those two axes because they are what the ear sorts on first: whether sound is
continuing or being interrupted, and whether it is intact or damaged. Pitch and
space are real dimensions too but read as *within* a family rather than between.

### The failure that mattered

Weighting purely by distance, the first render used **five of eight families in
eighteen segments**. OPEN, COLOURED and RUNG never appeared once.

The reason is structural rather than a tuning problem: the extremes are far from
everything, so they win every draw, while the middle families are far from
nothing. And the middle families are exactly the relief - **without OPEN there
is nothing to hear the damage against**, and unbroken extremity flattens into
its own sameness.

Fixed by giving each family a hunger that grows the longer it goes unused and
multiplies its weight. All eight appear now.

### Two smaller decisions

- **Segment lengths vary** (3.5-8 s) rather than sitting on a grid. A fixed

- **Transitions glide in the control signal, not the audio**, over 0.25 s. A
  hard switch on every parameter at once clicks; a slow ramp on all of them
  turns eight distinct families into one continuous morph.

## 29. Four presets: the disposition is the player's

> let's take a single performance and let that inform the layer of effects on
> our carrier. So let's do several, and then we'll have a selection of presets,
> so to speak.

His formulation from §22 applied to the eight families of §28, over a carrier
that is no longer the driver's own audio. The driver is never heard.

    WHEN     the driver's accents        discrete      segment boundaries
    WHAT     its state at that moment    categorical   which family
    HOW FAR  its readings                continuous    the dials inside it

Same 92-second carrier, four times:

    driver           accents  segments   mean   where the time went
    drums              138       25      3.7s   rung 17 detuned 17 ruined 17
    archtop            106       21      4.4s   rung 26 ruined 16 suspended 14
    piano               82       16      5.7s   open 20 suspended 18 detuned 16
    bass clarinet       77       18      5.1s   rung 21 detuned 16 ruined 14

**Nothing here was set.** The segmentation rate is the accent rate; the family
distribution is the state distribution. That the drummer segments every 3.7 s
and the bass clarinet every 5.1 s is a fact about how they played, and the
preset is a disposition rather than a setting.

Three things fell out that would have been hard to arrange deliberately:

- **The piano is the only preset where OPEN dominates** - 20 s, more than any
  family in any other preset. It leaves the carrier alone more than anyone else
  does.
- **The archtop holds RUNG for 26 s**, the longest single-family stretch of the
  four. Its own ring is what asks for resonance.
- **SPACED nearly vanishes under the bass clarinet** (3 s against the piano's
  7 s and the drummer's 11 s). Reverb length follows `decay`, and an instrument
  that sustains rather than decaying never raises the reading that opens it.

That last one is the clearest evidence so far that the mappings are doing
something real rather than decorating: a wind instrument gets less reverb than a
drum kit, and nobody decided that.

### One thing that had to be added

A family that has just played is damped to 0.35 for two segments. Without it a
driver sitting still produced the same family three or four times running -
correct behaviour from the weights and useless to listen to. Same shape of
problem as the hunger term in §28, at the other end: there, coverage; here,
repetition.

## 30. A correction: the four presets were the same preset

§29 reported that the presets differed by driver, and quoted numbers — piano
`open` 20 s, archtop `rung` 26 s, `spaced` nearly absent under the bass
clarinet. **Those were single-seed artefacts.** Running each driver over twelve
seeds:

    driver           open coloured  rung detuned ruined scatter suspend spaced
    piano           12+-9    8+-5  12+-8  14+-11  16+-8    6+-5   14+-9  15+-10
    drums            8+-4   11+-6  12+-5   13+-6  19+-5   10+-4   12+-5   14+-4
    archtop          8+-7   10+-7  12+-7   13+-6  16+-5    8+-5   15+-7   14+-7
    bass-clarinet    9+-7   10+-8  11+-7   13+-7  16+-7   14+-7   11+-7   11+-7

Every driver within one standard deviation of every other on every family. The
presets had no character; the differences were noise, and I reported them as
findings.

### Three causes, found by printing rather than by guessing

**Per-part normalisation.** Each driver's readings were stretched to fill 0–1
*over its own range*, which makes a quiet drummer and a loud one identical and a
bass clarinet exactly as bright as a cymbal. Fixed with an archive-wide
reference scale (`reference_scale.py`, 34 parts pooled). On it the instruments
genuinely separate:

    spectral flatness   drums 0.55   violin 0.29   archtop 0.13   piano 0.03
    spread              drums 0.88   violin 0.74   piano 0.51     bari 0.40
    busy                electronics 1.00   drums 0.32   piano 0.13

**That alone changed nothing.** The distributions stayed flat, which is what
made the second cause visible: `level` sits near 0.85 for every part, because
everyone plays at a similar loudness. Weighting RUINED by it added a large
constant and no information.

**And the floors swamped the readings.** A floor of 0.55 against a reading term
of 0.35 means the reading can move a weight by less than the floor already
guarantees. The floors existed to stop any family becoming impossible; they were
large enough that nothing was ever likely either. The recency damping at 0.35
pushed the same way.

### After

Floors cut to roughly a tenth, weights built from the readings that actually
separate instruments, the result raised to the power 2.2, damping softened to
0.62:

    driver           open coloured  rung detuned ruined scatter suspend spaced
    piano            5+-7    9+-9   5+-5  21+-10   0+-1   10+-5  36+-11  11+-9
    drums            1+-2    8+-5   3+-2   10+-6  27+-7   23+-5   16+-6  11+-4
    archtop          2+-3    6+-6   4+-4   21+-6    6+-4  20+-6   28+-4   9+-5
    bass-clarinet    1+-2    6+-8   9+-10  13+-9    9+-5  19+-10  29+-10  8+-7

**Piano RUINED 0 ± 1. Drums RUINED 27 ± 7.** Not a difference of means inside
the noise — a separation the noise cannot reach. And it is the right one: the
drum kit is the noisiest source in the archive and the piano the cleanest, and
noise is what asks for damage.

### What this cost and what it is worth

The two devices that broke it — generous floors and hard recency damping — were
both added deliberately, in §28 and §29, to guarantee coverage and to avoid
repetition. **They are anti-character devices, and they worked.** Every fix for
sameness in a stochastic chooser pushes toward uniformity, and uniformity is
exactly what a preset must not be.

The general lesson, and the one for the paper: a system whose output is drawn
from weights needs its weights *measured across seeds*, not inspected in one
render. A single render of a stochastic process is a sample, and reading
character off a sample is how you end up describing noise.

## 31. Two layers in series

> driven by the archtop and then driven by the bass clarinet layered on top of
> that. So two layers of processing.

Series rather than parallel, and the reading matters: "on top of that" means the
second layer processes **the output of the first**, not the same carrier again.
Mixed in parallel, two presets average into something less characterful than
either. In series they compose — the archtop rings the carrier, the bass
clarinet scatters *the ringing*, and that is a sound neither produces alone.

    layer 1  archtop        21 segments   detuned 32s, suspended 21s
    layer 2  bass clarinet  18 segments   scattered 37s, suspended 16s

**37 boundaries between them; 3 fall within half a second of each other.** The
rest cross, so the result changes roughly twice as often as either layer, at the
union of two players' accents.

One thing had to be discovered rather than assumed: at full strength the second
layer does not add to the first, it **replaces** it. Every family sets a wet/dry
mix, and the dry path is what carries the first layer's work — so a second layer
at mix 0.7 discards 70% of it. `--temper` scales the second pass's mixes; the
default 0.55 keeps the first layer audible underneath.

## 33. The ensemble granulates the processing

> lets Add the granular layer. Can this be driven by the data extraction? If so,
> can we layer all the datasets from a session?

Yes, and the multi-part half is the more interesting one. Five variants built:
each takes one already-processed render as its base and cuts grains out of it,
with **every part of one session** contributing.

    base                        session granulating it        grains   /s
    piano preset                piano arch tenor drums (4)      825    8.9
    archtop preset              piano bari arch drums (4)       803    8.7
    drums preset                bcl piano bari (3)              586    6.3
    bass clarinet preset        violin alto archtop (3)         892    9.7
    the two-layer stack         piano arch drums (3)            737    8.0

### Why a whole session and not parts from several

The parts of one session are **sample-aligned**. When the drummer, the pianist
and the saxophonist each place grains on their own onsets, those grains land in
the players' real relationship to each other — the interlocking, the leaving of
space, the piling up at moments they all moved. Three parts taken from three
different sessions would give three densities and no relationship.

### Why this layer can hold the ensemble at once

The processing chain has **one state**: it can be RUINED or SUSPENDED, not both,
so a preset must have a single driver. Grains are **additive**. Four players
contribute simultaneously with no conflict.

This is the first place in the engine where the whole ensemble is present *at
the same time* rather than by division of labour, and it took thirty-two entries
to find, because every previous stage had a single-state bottleneck in it.

### What makes it voices rather than density

Each player's readings shape their own grains, and one mapping carries most of
the weight: **a fixed seat in the stereo image, per player, held for the whole
render.** Without a place of their own the players sum into one cloud with more
events in it.

The measurement that shows it worked is channel correlation:

    g3  three winds and a piano     L/R correlation 0.32
    g1  four players inc. drums     0.67

The sparser ensemble separates further, which is what a sparser ensemble does in
a room.

Grain length follows each player's own `decay`, so a player whose sound dies
fast gets short grains and one that sustains gets long ones — **the grain
inherits how that instrument ends**. Brightness transposes, but only across
about a fifth: past that the grain stops being a window onto the base and
becomes a separate sound placed on top of it.

### The tension from §26, resolved by another route

§26 recorded that the plan called for granular *synthesis* as the clay while the
render J kept returning to had a *recording* as its clay, and put three options
to him. This is option (c) arriving without being chosen: the pebbly granular
texture, where **every grain is a piece of something someone played**, and the
material it is cut from has already been transformed by a fourth player's
reading of a fifth.

## 34. A catalogue, and handles you can say out loud

> can you develop a labeling or indexing type of system? So if there's a
> particular, for example, processing layer just to time containers, I can say,
> oh, I like that one. Let's put that in a favorites.

Fifty entries: 12 carriers, 30 processing layers, 8 granular stems.

    c   carrier      a single performance - session, part, in-point, length
    p   processing   a driver and a segmentation, over a bench carrier
    g   granular     a session's ensemble, grains only, no bed
    s   stack        two or more in series

### Why the names are meaningless

`p017` is a handle. A filename that describes its contents stops being true the
moment the contents change, and one long enough to describe a layer is too long
to say. Everything true lives in the index, where it can be corrected without
renaming a file - and the point of the whole exercise is being able to say a
thing's name after hearing it.

**Favourites and notes are only ever written by J**, never by a render. A
catalogue that promotes its own entries has an opinion; the value of this one is
that it does not.

### A layer is a recipe plus an audition

The recipe - driver, session, hold, seed - is the layer, and it re-applies to
any carrier. The 30-second audition only makes it judgeable. So `p017` is not a
file but a thing that can be put somewhere else, which is what makes stacking
and re-use possible at all.

All 30 processing layers are heard over the **same bench carrier**, so what
differs between them is the layer and not the material.

### Two decisions from J's own words

**Shorter windows.** The minimum a family may last is drawn per layer from 0.7
to 2.6 s instead of fixed at 3.0. The catalogue runs from 3 segments in 30 s
(`p025`, 10 s mean) to 15 (`p021`, 2.0 s mean).

**Granular layers as stems, no bed.** A layer with its own base baked in cannot
be stacked without dragging that base along.

### What the shelf costs

Fifty 30-second tracks do not fit under the 16 MB artifact ceiling at listening
quality. The page drops to 72 kbps, and that is right *here* in a way it would
not be in the room: **a shelf is for triage and the room is for judgement.**
Anything that survives triage gets rendered long and promoted.

The audio is gitignored, as `build/` is. The index and the recipes are the
deliverable; 296 MB of wavs is not.

## 35. A synthetic voice, and no sines

> This one I want to use synthesis. and we'll use granular as the base. […]
> Let's do FM grains and noise grains and sawtooth, things like that. Stuff with
> spectrum.

Everything else in the engine either processes a recording or cuts grains out of
one. This one makes sound, which makes it a **voice** rather than a layer.

Four grain types, weighted rather than evenly split: **FM 40%, filtered noise
26%, sawtooth 19%, pulse 15%**. FM carries the set because its index moves
between nearly-a-tone and a cloud of sidebands, and that move is the most
characterful thing available.

### Why the exclusion matters more than the inclusions

A sine has one partial, so there is nothing to granulate. A cloud of sines is a
cloud of the same thing, and **changing its density is audible only as a change
in loudness**. Every type here has internal structure, so density changes the
texture instead. That is what makes J's instruction - no sines, do a lot with
density - one instruction rather than two.

### Density is the instrument

    density   grains per second     from the driver's measured density
    gap       how clustered         from its flux: busy playing bunches them,
                                    still playing spreads them evenly
    overlap   how much they run     density x duration, compensated

The gaps are *drawn*, not gridded. On a fixed grid, density becomes a loudness
control; drawn from a distribution whose shape follows the flux, it stays a
spacing control.

Pitches follow the driver's own centroid, so the voice sits where that player
was sitting - not playing their notes, which it cannot know, but occupying their
region of the spectrum as they move through it.

Four built, and the densities are the players':

    electronics    786 grains   26.2 /s
    drums          552          18.4
    piano          490          16.3
    bass clarinet  478          15.9

### The clip that was already in the file

The first render came back with a peak of **exactly 1.000**. The score writes
int16, so that is clipping already baked in by the time it can be measured, and
a cloud of hundreds of grains has a crest factor no per-grain calculation
predicts - the 1/sqrt(overlap) compensation is correct for the *average* and
says nothing about the worst moment.

Fixed by rendering at 42% and normalising afterwards. Peaks now land 0.33-0.49
before normalisation. The general form: **when the render is destructive, leave
headroom rather than compute the right level.**

## 36. Processing form: the silences do the work

Built from the proposal in §35 — option (c), granulating the processing, with
option (a), the players' own silences, as its density.

Each stream is **one effect controlled by one performance**, admitted through
enveloped windows placed on that player's accents. Several streams sum over the
same carrier, each with its own windowing, so they cross rather than coincide.

### The first attempt was on 83% of the time

Windows on every accent, length 0.30 + activity x 2.6 s. With 1.3 accents per
second they overlapped into something continuous, which is not what was asked
for. The mistake was using the accents and **not the silences** — every accent
opened a window, whether or not the player was doing anything around it.

Adding a floor fixed it: an accent in a passage where the player's activity is
below 0.30 opens nothing at all. Then a quiet stretch produces no stream, which
is the difference between a stream that pulses and one that is genuinely absent
and genuinely returns.

    drums / ruined       45% on
    piano / spaced       18%
    archtop / rung       46%
    archtop / suspended  49%
    bass clarinet / detuned  13%
    piano / coloured     14%

Six streams, from 13% to 49%, and **not one of those numbers was chosen**. They
are how much those people were playing.

### Why there is one control and not two

The obvious way to make a stream intermittent is to pick a duration for on and a
duration for off, which means picking two numbers that have nothing behind them.
Here overlapping windows merge: dense playing runs them together into something
continuous, thin playing leaves them isolated, silence removes them. Continuous
and intermittent are the two ends of one control, which is the same shape as the
grains-to-freeze axis J named for the substrate, arriving at the formal level.

### The asymmetric envelope

Closing a window cuts whatever is still sounding, and for a reverb **what is
still sounding is the reverb**. So attack and release are per family:

    suspended  120 ms attack   2.00 s release
    spaced      90 ms          1.70 s
    rung        20 ms          0.90 s
    ruined       6 ms          0.18 s
    scattered    5 ms          0.15 s

And the release is exponential rather than linear, because a linear fade on a
decaying tail is audible as a fade and an exponential one is not.

### The pair J asked for

Four renders, two with a granular layer over the finished streams and two
without:

    s001  3 streams                          s002  the same, then granulated
    s003  4 streams                          s004  the same, then granulated

The granulation is the *same mechanism* as the base granular layers — a whole
session's ensemble, cut out of whatever it is given — which is what J said it
should be: "it will be the same as the one I'm using for the live base."

## 38. Order, and a null result worth keeping

Two granulations existed and only one could touch a recording: the synth grains
generate and read no audio at all; the ensemble grains read, but place one grain
per real onset at 2-10 a second. **A dense short-grain cloud made of a
recording did not exist.** `render_dense_grains.py` is the synth scheduler -
drawn gaps, 8-140 ms, 15-26 a second - driving grains that read out of a buffer.

That made it possible to put granulation *before* the effects for the first
time. Everything until now granulated after, so a grain was a window onto
already-transformed material; put first, the effects work on already-granular
material instead.

### The first matched pair could not tell them apart

    b  granulate then effects   rms -19.6 dB  crest 15.4  centroid 1549 Hz
    c  effects then granulate   rms -19.9      crest 16.5  centroid 1575

And on the measure that should have separated them - how abruptly the envelope
moves, which is where grain edges live - identical to two decimal places.

**The test could not have shown a difference.** The streams were on 32% of the
time over a dry bed at 0.50, so most of the output was untouched carrier either
way and the granulation dominated both. A null result from an experiment that
lacked the power to find anything is not evidence of no difference; it is
evidence of a bad experiment.

### Sharpened, they separate

No dry bed at all, streams at 84% on, and two families that smear time -
SUSPENDED and SPACED:

    b  granulate -> effects   level sd 9.3 dB   fast/slow modulation 1.96
    c  effects -> granulate   level sd 7.9 dB   fast/slow modulation 2.09

Small but consistent, and in a direction worth understanding. **Granulating
first keeps 1.4 dB more dynamic range.** Granulating a continuous smeared wash
cuts every grain from material of similar level, so the levels come out similar;
granulating first makes gaps that the reverb only partly fills, and the
underlying dynamics survive through it.

## 39. A gate on the bed, thrown mid-run

> can we also just have an option to put a gate on the effects? So it's kind of
> like a wet dry dial, and I guess that would be, like, all wet. so that if it's
> not being processed, you're not hearing the bed.

> But this should be a switch if possible to turn on and off during a run.

`--gate-at "0:off,10:on,20:off"`. Times and states, ramped over 12 ms because a
hard switch on a running signal clicks.

                        0-10 s    10-20 s   20-30 s
    gate open           10%       27%       84%
    gate thrown         10%       27%       22%

The two agree exactly where they should. The first third is dense enough that
gating removes almost nothing whether it is on or off; the middle third has the
gate open in both; and the last third - sparse playing, gate open in one and
shut in the other - is 84% silent against 22%. **The gate does most of its work
where the players are doing least**, which is what it is for.

## Themes, for whoever writes the paper

**Build the measurement before the fix.** Most of the day's real findings came
from benches, not from the detector. Two textbook techniques (the frequency max
filter, phase-based detection) were actively harmful here and only measurement
said so.

**Negative results carried more weight than positive ones.** The window-length
and phase-deviation dead ends are more transferable than the fix that worked,
because they explain *why* the obvious approaches fail on this material.

**Two of the three most valuable moments were the player listening**, not the
system measuring: the pulse ground truth that deleted a feature, and the
any-to-any reframing that tripled the archive.

**Distrust monotonic improvement.** The window-length curve kept improving and
was partly an artefact.

**A feature that cannot be calibrated should be removed.** Not re-thresholded.

**Superimposing and articulating are the two ends of one control, not two
techniques.** A layer that reads out of the layer beneath it is one thing with a
rhythm imposed on it; the same layer playing alongside is two things. How much
of the base survives underneath is the parameter that decides which, and it is
a musical decision.

**Borrowing a statistic from the playing is not borrowing the playing.** A
rate, a mean, a distribution can all be regenerated, and regenerating them is
what makes derived material still sound synthetic. Where the events actually
fell cannot be regenerated, and is the only part worth having.

**Build the comparison, let the ear supply the number.** Every value in MODES
was chosen by me and auditioned while a driver was moving - the condition under
which it is impossible to tell a good setting from a good moment. audition.py
holds everything still and sweeps one parameter. The mechanism is mine; the
number is not.

**When a reference is offered, ask which part of it is being pointed at.** I
took Risset literally - invert, retrograde - and built nine scalar operations.
The point was the *stage*, not the operations, and a step in it can be another
player. The literal reading was a strict subset of the intended one.

**A legible operation on unpredictable input beats a sophisticated one.**
Risset's transformations are each statable in a sentence - invert, scale,
iterate, delay. That is what makes them audible as transformations rather than
as noise.

**The productive axis was not what answers the playing, but what shapes it.**
Retrieval spent a day asking which fragment should respond, and the answer was
weakly determined. Cross-driving asks what a piece of material should *become*
and takes the answer from another player who was in the room. The sample-exact
alignment that made it possible was guaranteed for retrieval, which needed it
less.

**A palette built one element at a time is unbalanced in whatever dimension
nobody was considering.** For the grain modes that was duration - not a property
of any single mode, but of the comparison between them. The correction already
existed elsewhere in the repo and nobody had carried it across.

**Continuous modulation gives motion; switching gives variety.** They are not
substitutes. The same complaint - "it doesn't change enough" - arrived twice, at
the grain and then at the processing, and both times the fix was to add a second,
faster rate rather than to modulate the existing one harder.

**Ask which parameters are measured and which are merely left at a default.**
The phrase-scale clouds took density and loudness from real playing and used a
sine grain because nobody had chosen otherwise. The complaint that followed was
entirely about the parameter with no source.

**The best routings replace an arbitrary parameter, not a good one.** Reverb
time and damping were always guessed; the archive has a measured answer for
both. That is a better test for "is this worth wiring up" than novelty.

**Data already collected can validate a design decision made years earlier.**
The cloud engine's density/loudness decoupling was adopted on theoretical
grounds from Roads. Reading the frame features at phrase scale showed 24% of
real phrases do exactly that. Nothing new was recorded to find it.
